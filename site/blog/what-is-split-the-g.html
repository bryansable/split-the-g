Somewhere in most pubs where stout is poured, someone is staring at their glass with a look of deep concentration usually reserved for surgery or parallel parking. They lift it, take one long, careful sip, set it down and lean in. Then either a small cheer goes up or a friend says "nowhere near" with enormous satisfaction. That is Split the G, and once you have seen it you will notice it everywhere.

The basic idea

The classic stout pint glass has a G printed on the side, a little below the rim. The challenge is to take a single sip from a fresh, settled pint so that the line where the dark beer meets the creamy head ends up running straight through the middle of that letter. Specifically, through the horizontal bar of the G, the little shelf that sticks inwards on the right-hand side.

Get the line there and you have split the G. Leave it above the bar and you were too timid. Drag it below and you were greedy, or at least thirsty. Either way, your friends will tell you.

The rules, as most people play them

There is no governing body, thankfully, but the rules that turn up at most tables are pretty consistent:

  • One sip. Not two. Not a sip and then a "tidy-up" sip. One continuous go at it.
  • A fresh, settled pint. The beer should have finished its surge and the head should be sitting firm before you start. Attempting it on a pint that is still cascading is cheating yourself more than anyone else.
  • No nudging afterwards. Once the glass goes down, it stays down. No tilting it to "see better", no swirling, no blowing on the head.
  • Judge at eye level. The line is read from the side, level with the glass. Looking down from above makes almost anything look like a split.

That last one causes more arguments than the other three put together, which is part of why we built a way to measure it. More on that below.

Where it came from

Nobody can point to a first person who did it, and anyone who claims to is probably telling a pub story. The more honest version is that it seems to have been a quiet habit among stout drinkers for a long time. The G sits in a natural spot on the glass, people noticed, and some of them started aiming for it.

What changed was social media. In the early 2020s, short videos of people attempting the split, and mostly failing, started doing the rounds, and the habit turned into something with a name and a following. Bars began to see groups of friends filming each other's first sips. It went from a private bit of fun to something closer to a party trick that everyone has an opinion about.

Why people like it so much

Part of the appeal is that it looks easy and isn't. The target is right there, you can see exactly where you need to end up, and yet your mouth is a very poor measuring instrument. You cannot see the line while you are drinking, the glass is tilted, and the head plays tricks on you.

It also costs nothing extra. You were having the pint anyway. The game lasts about five seconds, involves everybody at the table, and produces a verdict that people will happily argue about for the next twenty minutes. That is a lot of entertainment for one mouthful.

And it rewards patience. A good split depends on a properly poured, properly settled pint, which tends to make people a bit more appreciative of the pour itself. If you want the long version of why that matters, have a read of the two-part pour.

Variations and house rules

Like any pub game, it has picked up local flavour. A few you might come across:

  • Best of the round. Everybody attempts it on their own pint and the closest wins bragging rights. No forfeits needed; being the worst is punishment enough.
  • Top or bottom of the bar. Strict players insist the line must sit between the top and bottom edges of the G's bar. Generous ones accept anything touching it.
  • Any glass. Plenty of pubs serve stout in plain glasses with no G at all. People still play, guessing where the G would be. This is where the arguments really get going.
  • The sober version. Some people play with sparkling water or an alcohol-free stout. It is the same skill, and nobody can blame the beer.

A sensible house rule worth adopting everywhere: the game is about one sip, not about drinking more or faster. Nobody should be lining up pints to have another go. If you want to make a night of it, hosting a G-off gives you a way to keep it fun without turning it into a drinking race. And, obviously, it is for people of legal drinking age, unless you are playing with water.

How the app turns an argument into a number

The trouble with judging by eye is that everyone at the table sees a slightly different line, from a slightly different angle, with a slightly different amount of loyalty to the person who drank it. So we built Split The G, a free app that runs in your phone's browser and scores the sip with the camera.

You point your phone at the glass with the whole thing in view. The app finds the rim, the base and the walls on its own, and if it gets one wrong you can drag the rim or base guide into place. Then you take your sip, put the glass down, and the app reads the liquid line at the front of the glass live. Tap capture and you get a score.

Because it measures everything as a fraction of the glass height, it does not matter how big your glass is or how far away you are holding the phone. A line inside the bar of the G scores 100 and you get the verdict you came for. Miss, and you lose points depending on how far off you were, along with a note telling you roughly how many millimetres too high or too low you landed. That last bit is surprisingly useful if you actually want to get better. The full detail is in how the app scores your sip.

If your glass has no G on it, the app draws a "ghost G" roughly where it would sit on the classic tulip glass, about 30% of the way down from the rim. If you do have the real thing, there is an option to switch the ghost off and use the printed G. The camera frames are analysed on the phone itself and never uploaded, so you are not sending pictures of your Friday night anywhere.

Give it a go

Next time you are at a table with a properly poured stout in front of you, let it settle, hold your phone level with the glass and see how you do. Open Split The G, take your one sip, and find out whether you really split it or whether your mate was right all along. If you would rather skip the alcohol, the sparkling edition plays exactly the same way with a glass of fizzy water.
