Ask for a pint in Dublin and you get one thing. Ask for a pint in Chicago and you get something noticeably smaller. Ask for a pint in parts of Australia and you might get a puzzled look, a schooner, or something else depending on which state you're standing in. Ask for one in Munich and the barman will politely bring you half a litre and assume you meant that.

The word "pint" has travelled a long way and picked up local habits along the route. Here's a tour of what it means in different places, why the differences exist, and why, for the purposes of splitting the G, the volume in the glass matters a great deal less than you'd think.

The imperial pint

In the UK and Ireland, a pint is the imperial pint: 568 millilitres, or 20 imperial fluid ounces. It's the measure the classic tulip stout glass is built around, and it's the one most people picture when they hear the word.

In the UK, draught beer and cider are still legally sold in pints and fractions of a pint, which is why you'll see halves and, in some places, two-thirds measures on the menu. Ireland uses the same imperial pint for draught beer, and asking for "a pint" or "a glass" (which usually means a half) is standard bar language there.

The US pint

An American pint is 473 millilitres, or 16 US fluid ounces. That makes it roughly a sixth smaller than the imperial pint, which is the kind of difference you notice when you've been drinking both.

Why the gap? The short version is that both systems grew out of older English measures, but they settled on different gallons. The US kept a gallon based on an older English wine gallon, while Britain redefined its gallon in the nineteenth century when it standardised the imperial system. A pint is an eighth of a gallon in both, so different gallons mean different pints. On top of that, the fluid ounces aren't quite the same size either, and the imperial pint has 20 of them while the US pint has 16. Historians of measurement can take you much deeper into this; for a bar conversation, "different gallons" will do.

There's a wrinkle in American bars too. The pint is a common glass size, but not every glass called a pint holds 16 fluid ounces when filled to a sensible level. Which brings us neatly to a long-running grumble.

The short pour

Anywhere beer is sold by the pint, someone has complained that their pint wasn't one. In the UK, the argument is usually about the head: is the foam part of the pint, or should the liquid itself reach the full measure? Guidance and practice have varied over the years, and a lot of pubs use oversized "lined" glasses with a fill line so the head can sit above the measure. In the US, the complaint is more often about glassware: the popular straight-sided "shaker" glass is sometimes used for pours that come in under 16 ounces once there's foam on top.

We won't settle this here. But if you've ever looked at a glass and felt short-changed, you're in large and grumbling company.

Canada: a bit of both

Canada officially went metric decades ago, but pub language didn't entirely follow. A "pint" on a Canadian menu might be an imperial-sized pour, a US-sized pour, or something in between, and practice varies by province and by bar. Some places list the volume in millilitres or ounces, which is the friendliest thing a menu can do. If it matters to you, ask.

Australia: depends where you're standing

Australia is where beer measures get properly regional. Glass names change from state to state, and the same name can mean different sizes in different places. You'll hear schooner, middy, pot, handle and pint, among others. A schooner is a familiar order in some states, a pot or middy in others, and in some places a "pint" may not mean the 568 millilitre imperial measure at all.

We'd rather not hand you a table of exact figures, because the details vary by state, have shifted over time and are a reliable source of arguments between Australians. The practical advice is the same as in Canada: when you're new in town, ask what the sizes are, or look for the volume on the menu.

Continental Europe: just say the number

Much of mainland Europe skipped the whole business and went straight to litres. In Germany, Austria, Belgium, the Netherlands and plenty of other countries, you'll commonly see 0.5 litre, 0.4 litre, 0.3 litre and 0.25 litre glasses, often with a printed fill line on the glass showing exactly where the measure sits. Half a litre is about 500 millilitres, so it's a bit smaller than an imperial pint and a bit larger than a US one.

That printed line is a lovely, honest bit of design. It also happens to be the same idea as the G: a mark on the glass that tells you where the liquid should be.

Why the G doesn't care about volume

Here's the interesting part for anyone playing. On the classic tulip pint glass, the G in the logo sits roughly 30% of the way down from the rim. When people talk about splitting the G, they're really talking about drinking the glass down to a particular fraction of its height, not a particular number of millilitres.

That matters because glasses vary wildly. A US pint glass is a different shape from an imperial tulip. A half-litre continental glass is different again. A Canadian bar might pour into whatever it has on the shelf. If the game depended on volume, every glass would need its own target, and the comparison between friends in different countries would be meaningless.

Instead, the question is always the same: how far down the glass did your line land, as a share of the glass's height? That's why the shape of the tulip glass, which you can read about in the tulip pint glass, sets the target, and the volume is almost beside the point.

How the app handles any glass

Split The G leans into this. When you point your phone at a glass, it finds the rim and the base and measures everything as a fraction of the height between them. That's why your distance from the glass and the size of the glass don't matter. A 568 millilitre tulip and a 473 millilitre shaker are both just "100% tall" as far as the maths is concerned.

If you aren't using the original branded glass, the app draws a ghost G at the spot where the real one would sit, so you have something to aim at. If you do have the real glass, there's an "Official glass" toggle that hides the ghost so it doesn't sit on top of the printed G. The score itself is based on how far your line is from the bar of the G, in percentage points of glass height, and the result also tells you roughly how many millimetres too high or low you were. It's worth saying that differently shaped glasses do make the game feel different. A glass that flares outwards near the top means a sip of a given size moves the line less there than it would lower down. A straight-sided glass is more predictable. None of that is unfair; it's just part of learning your glass.
