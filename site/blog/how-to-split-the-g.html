Splitting the G is one of those things that looks like luck until you have done it a few times on purpose. There is real technique to it. Not a lot, and none of it difficult, but enough that the person who knows what they are doing will beat the person who just goes for it, most nights.

If you are new to the whole idea, start with what Split the G actually is. If you know the rules and want to stop landing two centimetres under the bar, read on.

Wait for the pint to settle. Properly.

This is the single biggest thing, and the one people skip because they are eager. A freshly topped stout is still moving. The bubbles are still rising, the head is still firming up and the boundary between beer and foam is still drifting. If you drink from it in that state, the line you leave behind will carry on moving after you put the glass down, and usually not in your favour.

Give it until the beer is fully dark and still all the way up to the head, and the head itself has a clean, defined bottom edge. If you are not sure, wait another half a minute. Nobody ever lost a split by being too patient. There is more on why this matters in the two-part pour.

Grip and tilt

Hold the glass in your dominant hand, around the lower half, with a relaxed grip. Squeezing it tight makes you more likely to jerk the glass when you finish. Hold it so the G faces away from you; you will not be able to see it while you drink anyway, and it means your friends get a clear view of the result.

Tilt the glass smoothly and steadily. The aim is a single, even draw rather than a series of little gulps. Most bad splits come from stop-start drinking, where you pause, panic, then take another bit to make up for it. One smooth tilt in, one smooth tilt back.

How big a sip is it, really?

Bigger than most first-timers think. The G sits roughly 30% of the way down from the rim on the classic tulip glass. That glass is widest near the top, so the top 30% of its height holds a noticeably bigger share of the volume than 30% of the bottom would. Some of that space is head, which is mostly foam and goes down easily, but there is a fair bit of actual beer between the rim and the bar of the G as well.

In practice that means a proper, confident mouthful. Not a polite sip you would take from a cup of tea, and not a gulp either. If you have only ever sipped stout delicately, your first attempts will almost certainly finish too high. That is fine. Too high is the safe direction to miss in, and the next attempt will tell you how much more you need.

Breathing

It sounds daft, but take a breath before you start. Trying to take a long mouthful while you are halfway through exhaling is how people end up stopping early. Breathe in, tilt, draw steadily, and stop when you have the amount you planned. Then lower the glass slowly and set it down flat.

Feel versus sight

You cannot see the line while you drink. Anyone who tells you they can is either holding the glass at a strange angle or lying. So the split is mostly judged by feel: how much is in your mouth, how long you have been drawing, how far you have tilted the glass.

The useful habit is to decide your sip before you start rather than making it up as you go. Think "one full mouthful" and commit to it. Adjusting mid-sip almost always goes wrong because you have no information to adjust with.

Why the head matters

A thick, firm head helps in two ways. It makes the boundary easier to read, both for your eyes and for a camera, because there is a sharp change from dark beer to pale foam. It also stays put, so the line you leave is the line that gets judged. A thin, collapsing head gives you a fuzzy, drifting boundary and endless arguments. If you want to understand where that head comes from, why stout is so creamy covers the nitrogen side of things.

Practise with water

You do not need a pint to get your sip calibrated. A glass of water or sparkling water, filled to near the top, teaches you how much a mouthful is relative to the height of the glass. Mark where the G would sit, about 30% of the way down, and have a few goes. It is the same motion, and it costs nothing but a refill from the tap. The Split The G app has a sparkling edition for exactly this, and it works with any glass by drawing a ghost G where the real one would be.

The common mistakes

  • The too-shallow sip. By far the most common. People underestimate the volume and finish well above the bar. Be bolder than feels natural.
  • Tilting the glass to check. Once it is down, it is down. Picking it up and tipping it towards you to "have a look" moves the liquid and the head, and the line you see afterwards is not the line you left.
  • Judging from above. Looking down into the glass makes the line look higher on the far side and lower on the near side. Get down level with the glass, or hold the phone level with it.
  • Drinking before it settles. Covered above, but it bears repeating because it is the one everyone does.
  • Going again. If you missed, you missed. A second "correction" sip is a different game, and a less impressive one.

Using the score to calibrate

The most useful thing about measuring your attempts is the direction and size of the miss. When the app reads your line, it does not just give you a number; it tells you roughly how many millimetres too high or too low you landed, with a nudge like "4 mm too low. Smaller sip next time."

That is the feedback your mouth has been missing. After two or three goes you start to learn what "three millimetres more" feels like, and that is where the real improvement comes from.
