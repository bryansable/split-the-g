The whole point of splitting the G is one careful sip and a lot of squinting at a glass. Nothing about that requires alcohol. The fun is in the judgement, the steady hand and the small, ridiculous tension of putting the glass down and waiting to see where the line landed. A drink with no alcohol in it can carry all of that perfectly well.

Plenty of people at any given table aren't drinking. Some are driving. Some are under the legal drinking age and there with family. Some are pregnant, on medication, training for something, doing a dry month, or simply don't fancy it tonight and would rather not explain why. A game that only works if you're holding a pint of stout leaves those people as spectators. That never sat right, which is why the app has a sparkling edition.

What the sparkling edition is

The sparkling edition is the same game, played with sparkling water, wearing an ice-blue look instead of the stout colours. The scoring is identical: get the liquid line inside the bar of the G and you score 100. Miss, and you drop points by how far off you were. You still get told how many millimetres high or low you landed, and you can still share a result card.

When you first open Split The G, it asks whether you're of legal drinking age. If you answer no, it doesn't wave you away. It offers the sparkling edition instead, so you can play along with everyone else. And you don't need to answer no to use it: anyone can choose the sparkling edition, whatever their age or whatever is in their glass. It's not a children's version or a consolation prize. It's just the game, minus the booze.

Water is harder than stout, and that's interesting

Here is the slightly unexpected thing. Stout is the easiest drink to play with, and clear water is one of the trickiest. That's not a design flaw; it's physics, and it makes for a good challenge.

A well-poured stout has a dark body and a thick, pale head. The line between them is high-contrast and the head sits on top like a lid. When you sip, the dark liquid drops and the line is obvious to both your eye and a camera. (If you're curious why the head behaves the way it does, why stout is so creamy goes into the nitrogen and the bubbles.)

Sparkling water has none of that. No head to speak of, no dark body, just clear liquid in a clear glass. What gives the level away is the meniscus: the thin curve where the water's surface meets the glass. Look side-on at a glass of water and you'll see a slightly brighter or darker band at the surface, curving up a touch at the edges. That band is what the app looks for when there's no colour contrast to work with.

Bubbles complicate things

Sparkling water is restless. Bubbles cling to the inside of the glass, rise in streams and sit in a loose ring at the surface. Just after pouring, the surface can be fizzing so much that the line is genuinely fuzzy. Give it a little time to calm down before you take your sip, and again before you capture the reading. A few seconds of patience makes a real difference.

Ice is another troublemaker. Cubes float at the surface and break up the meniscus, and they melt during the game, which nudges the level upwards. If you want a fair reading, go without ice or use very little, and keep the drink chilled beforehand instead.

How to help the camera with clear drinks

  • Use a plain background, ideally something darker than the glass. A dark wall or a jacket draped over a chair makes the meniscus stand out.
  • Keep the phone level with the liquid, not looking down from above. From above, the surface is nearly invisible.
  • Wipe condensation off the front of the glass. Cold sparkling water fogs a glass quickly, and fog sits right where you need clarity.
  • Avoid bright lights directly behind the glass, which turn the whole thing into glare.

There's more on light and angles in how to photograph a pint, and almost all of it applies to water too.

Alcohol-free stout

If you want the look and feel of the original without the alcohol, alcohol-free stouts are now widely available, both on draught in some pubs and in cans. They vary. Some pour with a dense, creamy head that's very close to the full-strength version, particularly the ones packaged or dispensed with nitrogen. Others produce a looser, quicker-fading foam that behaves more like an ordinary carbonated beer.

For the game, the head is what matters. A thick, stable head gives you the same sharp line as the full-strength stout, so scoring is just as easy. A thinner head that settles fast still works, but the boundary may look less crisp. Either way, play it like a normal stout. Note that alcohol-free drinks in some countries can still contain a very small amount of alcohol, so check the label if that matters to you.

Other drinks that work

The app doesn't care what's in the glass, only where the line is. A few that play nicely:

  • Cola. Dark body, a brief foamy head on pouring, then a clear surface. Once the fizz settles, the dark liquid against the glass makes a clean line.
  • Iced coffee with a layer of cream. Probably the closest look to a stout you'll get without a tap. Let it settle so the layers separate.
  • Soda water or tonic. Same rules as sparkling water: patience with the bubbles and a dark background.
  • Fruit juice or squash. Coloured liquid shows up well; the line is usually easy to read.

Use any glass you like. The app draws a ghost G where the G would sit on the classic tulip glass, roughly 30% of the way down from the rim, so a tumbler or a jam jar works fine. Everything is measured as a fraction of the glass height, so a short glass and a tall glass are equally fair.

Making a mixed table feel like one game

The best nights are the ones where nobody is sitting out. A few small things help.

First, don't make a fuss. If someone's playing with water, that's just what they're playing with. Nobody needs to explain their glass. The question "go on, why aren't you drinking?" is rarely as harmless as it sounds.

Second, put everyone on the same leaderboard. In the app, friends join through an invite link and the daily G-off leaderboard resets at midnight. A 100 with sparkling water is a 100. If anything, it's harder to earn, so the water drinkers may well deserve a bit of extra respect.

Third, if you're running a proper night of it, the house rules in how to host a G-off work the same with any drink. One sip per round, whatever's in the glass.

And for whoever is driving: you can be the best player at the table without a drop of alcohol, and you get everyone home. That's a decent evening's work.

Try it

Pour a glass of something fizzy, let it settle, and open Split The G. Pick the sparkling edition, line up the glass, take one careful sip and see how close you get. It's free, it runs in your phone's browser, and the camera frames never leave your phone.
