Put a lager and a draught stout side by side and they could hardly look more different. One is bright, fizzy and full of rising streams of bubbles. The other arrives as a churning tan cloud that slowly clears into black beer under a thick, smooth cap of cream. Most of that difference comes down to one thing: the gas.

Two gases, two personalities

Almost all beer contains carbon dioxide, produced by yeast during fermentation. Carbon dioxide dissolves well in beer, especially cold beer, and comes out gradually as bubbles once the pressure is released. That is the prickle on your tongue and the steady fizz you see in a glass of lager.

Nitro stouts are served with a gas mix that is mostly nitrogen, with a smaller share of carbon dioxide. Nitrogen behaves very differently. It is far less willing to dissolve in beer, so only a small amount goes in, and it is eager to come back out. That reluctance turns out to be the secret to the texture.

Because there is less carbon dioxide in the beer, it tastes less sharp and less fizzy. Because the nitrogen comes out of solution fast, in a great many very small bubbles, you get a soft, dense foam instead of a loose, bubbly one.

Why the bubbles are so small

A draught stout tap usually forces the beer through a small plate with tiny holes before it reaches the glass. That violent squeeze knocks the gas out of the beer all at once and breaks it into masses of fine bubbles. The result is the cloudy, cream-coloured swirl you see when the pint is first poured.

Small bubbles matter for two reasons. They make the head feel smooth and velvety rather than frothy. And they are much more stable. In a foam made of big bubbles, the bubbles merge, pop and collapse quickly. A foam made of tiny, tightly packed nitrogen bubbles holds together for a long time, which is why a good stout head can still be sitting there when you are halfway down the glass.

The surge, and why it seems to go the wrong way

The part everybody films is the surge, sometimes called the cascade. Watch the side of a freshly poured stout and the bubbles appear to sink, drifting downwards in waves while the beer clears from the bottom up. Bubbles are supposed to rise, so what is going on?

They are rising. The bubbles in the middle of the glass float upwards and drag beer up with them. That beer has to go somewhere, so it flows back down along the walls of the glass, carrying the smaller bubbles near the edge downwards with it. What you see from the outside is that downward current at the wall. The shape of the glass plays a part too, which is one reason the tulip pint glass shows it off so nicely. There is more on that in the tulip pint glass.

The surge is also why the two-part pour exists. You need to give the bubbles time to rise out of the beer and build the head before the glass is filled to the top.

What about cans?

Getting that effect without a pub tap is trickier, which is where the widget comes in. Many canned nitro stouts contain a small hollow plastic capsule, usually a ball or a disc, with a tiny hole in it. In general terms, the can is sealed with a little liquid nitrogen added and under pressure, which pushes gas and some beer into the widget. When you open the can, the pressure in the can drops suddenly, and the widget squirts its gas out through that tiny hole into the beer. That jet does a similar job to the tap's restrictor plate, kicking off the tiny bubbles that produce the surge and head.

The usual advice with widget cans is to pour the whole can into a glass in one go rather than drinking from the can, and to pour fairly firmly. The surge needs a glass to happen in, and you will not see much of it through aluminium.

Why the head holds

A good stout head is dense enough that you can sometimes see it hold a shape for a while. A few things help it last:

  • Bubble size. Small bubbles are more stable than large ones, as above.
  • Proteins in the beer. Beer contains proteins from the malt and roasted barley that help bubble walls hold together.
  • Less carbon dioxide. Lagers keep releasing bubbles that disturb the foam. A stout's gentler carbonation leaves the head alone.
  • A clean glass. Grease and residue break foam down quickly. Bars that look after their glassware tend to serve better heads.

Temperature and serving

Temperature affects how the gas behaves. Colder beer holds gas more readily, and the settle can take a little longer; slightly warmer beer releases it more freely. Pubs often offer an "extra cold" option, and you will find people with strong feelings on both sides. The colder version can feel smoother; the standard one often has more flavour coming through. Neither is wrong, and it is worth trying both rather than arguing about it.

What does matter is that the beer is served consistently and the pint is given time to settle before it is handed over. A rushed pint is a rushed pint whatever the temperature.

Why the head helps you split the G

For the purposes of Split the G, the creamy head is a gift. The line you are trying to place through the bar of the G is the boundary between the dark beer and the pale head. With a nitro stout, that boundary is unusually sharp: near-black on one side, off-white on the other. It is easy for people to see and easy for a camera to read.

That is why a stout with a thick, settled head is the easiest drink to score. When you use Split The G, the app looks at the front of the glass and reads where that liquid line sits as a fraction of the glass height, then compares it to the bar of the G. The head itself does not count; it is the beer below it, and where the beer stops, that matters. Clear drinks work too, since the app looks for the meniscus line instead, but a crisp, dark-against-cream boundary is about as good as it gets.

A thin, collapsing head, on the other hand, gives a blurrier boundary that keeps moving. Another good reason to let the pint settle before you start.
