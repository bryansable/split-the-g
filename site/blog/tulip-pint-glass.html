Most people never think about the glass. It arrives full, it leaves empty, and in between it is just the thing you hold. But if you play Split the G, the glass stops being furniture and becomes the playing field. Its shape decides how big your sip needs to be, where the target sits, and why your mate's "easy" glass at the other pub felt like cheating. So it is worth a proper look.

The three glasses you will meet at the bar

Walk into pubs on either side of the Irish Sea, or across the Atlantic, and you will mostly be handed one of three shapes.

  • The nonic. A straight-ish glass with a ring-shaped bulge a couple of inches below the rim. The name is usually explained as "no nick": the bulge stops glasses chipping against each other when they are stacked, and it gives your hand something to grip. It is the workhorse of British pubs.
  • The shaker. Straight sides, tapering gently towards the base, no bulge at all. Common in American bars, partly because it stacks neatly and survives a busy dishwasher. Plain, honest, a little dull.
  • The tulip. Narrow at the foot, swelling outward as it rises, then curving gently back in towards the rim. This is the one the G is printed on, and the one most people picture when they hear the name of the game.

There are plenty of others, from dimpled mugs to stemmed goblets, and many breweries commission a glass of their own. But those three cover most pints poured in most pubs.

Why the tulip is shaped the way it is

A tulip pint is not curvy for decoration. The narrow base keeps the bottom of the drink compact, so the pour has somewhere to settle. The swell in the middle gives the liquid room to move as the gas works its way out, which matters a great deal with a nitrogenated stout and its famous cascade. If you want the full story of that, our piece on why stout is so creamy covers the bubbles in detail.

The inward curve near the top does two jobs. It helps hold the head together as a tidy, dense cap rather than letting it spread thin across a wide surface, and it funnels a little aroma towards your nose as you drink. Whether you can taste the difference is a conversation for a long evening, but the shape is clearly built around a drink with a thick head and a slow settle.

Why breweries bother with branded glassware

A brewery printing its logo on a glass is partly advertising, of course. Every pint on a table is a little billboard. But there is more to it than that. A branded glass lets the brewer control the stage on which the drink is presented: the shape, the volume, how much room the head has, and where the eye lands. Some glasses carry fill lines or markings that help staff pour consistently. A good branded glass is a quiet instruction manual.

The G on the tulip is a nice example. It is a logo, yes, but it also happens to sit at a height that turns a single mouthful into a target. Nobody needs to claim it was designed as a game for the game to exist. Drinkers found it on their own.

Why the G sits about 30% down

On the classic tulip pint, the G sits roughly 30% of the way down from the rim. The horizontal bar in the middle of the letter, the bit you are aiming for, sits in that zone. Once a properly poured pint has settled, the line between the dark body and the creamy head usually lands somewhere above the G, and one decent mouthful takes the liquid down to it.

That is the whole game. Drink until the line between stout and head cuts through the middle of the letter, and you have split it. Our practical guide to the first sip gets into technique, but the glass is what makes the target possible at all.

The curve that makes a sip bigger than it looks

Here is the bit that catches people out. Because a tulip glass is widest towards the top, the liquid between the rim and the G is not 30% of the drink. It is more. Each centimetre near the top of the glass holds more than a centimetre near the narrow base, because the glass is wider there.

In practice this means the sip needed to reach the G is bigger than your eye tells you. You look at the gap, think "that's a polite mouthful", and come up short. On a straight shaker glass, height and volume line up much more neatly, so the same visual instinct works better. On a tulip, the relationship between "how far down" and "how much I drank" is not a straight line. The first few millimetres cost the most liquid.

There is a second wrinkle. A stout's head sits on top and rides down as you drink, so you are drinking the liquid underneath the foam and judging the line between the two. Players who switch between glass shapes learn to recalibrate quickly, and the ones who do not tend to blame the barman.

Playing with any glass: the ghost G

Not everyone has the branded glass to hand, and plenty of pubs serve stout in whatever is clean. That is why Split The G draws a "ghost G" on screen at the height where the letter would sit, about 30% down from the rim of whatever glass you are holding. The app finds the rim and base itself, so the target scales to your glass rather than assuming a fixed size.

If you do have the real thing, flick the "Official glass" toggle and the ghost disappears, leaving you to aim for the printed G. Either way the score is worked out the same way, as a fraction of the glass height, so a nonic, a shaker or a tulip are judged on equal terms. Bear in mind that the curve lesson above still applies: on a wide-topped glass you will need a bigger sip to get there.

Keeping a glass "beer clean"

A clean glass and a "beer clean" glass are not the same thing. A glass can look spotless and still carry a thin film of grease, detergent or rinse aid. That film affects the drink more than you might expect.

The giveaway is bubbles clinging to the sides. In a properly clean glass, bubbles rise freely to the top. In a glass with residue, they find tiny spots to grip and sit there in little clusters on the inside walls. The head may also collapse faster, because oils and soap break up foam. A beer clean glass tends to leave rings of lacing down the inside as you drink, a sort of tidemark of each sip.

If you are pouring at home:

  • Wash glasses separately from greasy plates and pans, using a small amount of detergent.
  • Rinse thoroughly in clean water so no soap is left behind.
  • Let them air dry upside down on a rack rather than wiping with a tea towel, which can leave lint and whatever the towel last touched.
  • Give the glass a quick rinse in cold water just before pouring.
  • Try the sheeting test: wet the inside and watch. If the water runs off in an even sheet, good. If it breaks into beads and droplets, there is still something on the surface.

A clean glass also makes the game fairer. Clinging bubbles and a smeared wall make the liquid line harder to see, for you and for the camera.

Give your glass a go

Whatever is in the cupboard, it will do. Pour something you enjoy, or reach for sparkling water and play the alcohol-free sparkling edition, and let the app show you where the G would sit on your glass. One sip, one score, and a new respect for the shape you have been holding all along. If you are of legal drinking age and playing with stout, the usual rule applies: it is a game about one good mouthful, not a race to the bottom.
