Pub games have a long and mostly honourable history. Darts, dominoes, quizzes, bar billiards, the odd argument over a crossword. The best of them give a group something to do with their hands and their attention, which is exactly what a good night out needs. The worst of them are thinly disguised ways to get people drunk quickly, and those tend to end with someone being sick in a taxi.

Split the G belongs firmly in the first camp, and we'd like to keep it there. This is a friendly guide to enjoying it, and pub games generally, without the evening going sideways. It is not a lecture. You're an adult; you know how a pub works. These are just the habits that make the good nights good.

The game is one sip, on purpose

Splitting the G is about a single, controlled mouthful. You take one sip from a fresh pint, put the glass down, and see where the line landed. That's it. The skill is in judgement and restraint, not volume. A sip that's too big overshoots the G just as surely as one that's too timid falls short. If you want the details, how to split the G covers technique.

So there's no version of this game where drinking faster or more makes you better at it. Quite the opposite. Your hands get less steady, your judgement drifts, and your scores go the wrong way. The game rewards the person who's still sharp.

Don't turn it into a drinking game

Every so often someone suggests a forfeit: miss the G and you have to finish the pint. Please don't. That turns a game of precision into a race, and races with alcohol go badly. It also pressures people who'd rather pace themselves into drinking more than they meant to.

If you want stakes, use stakes that aren't drinks. The lowest scorer gets the crisps, picks the next song, or has to tell a story. Bragging rights on the leaderboard are usually enough. Running a proper G-off for a group? The rules in how to host a G-off are built around one attempt per person per pint, which keeps things sensible.

Pace yourself

A pint is meant to be drunk over a conversation, not over a minute. A well-poured stout takes a while to arrive anyway, and there's no prize for finishing first. Let the game take up a small part of the evening, not set its rhythm. You don't need a new pint for every round; you can take your sip, score it, and then just enjoy the rest of the drink at your own speed.

Water between rounds

It's an old pub habit for good reason. A glass of water alongside each drink, or between them, slows the pace down naturally and keeps you better hydrated. Most pubs in the UK and Ireland will give you tap water for free if you ask. Bar staff are generally pleased when someone asks.

Eat something

Drinking on an empty stomach makes everything hit faster. A proper meal beforehand is ideal, but even a bag of crisps and a packet of nuts is better than nothing. If the pub does food, that's a fine excuse to make an evening of it.

Know the guidelines where you live

We're not going to tell you how many drinks is "safe". That's not our place, the guidance differs by country, and it changes as the evidence develops. Your local health service publishes official advice on low-risk drinking, and it's worth a look:

  • In the UK, the NHS has guidance on alcohol units and lower-risk drinking.
  • In Ireland, the HSE publishes its own guidance on low-risk drinking.
  • In the United States, the CDC has information on alcohol use and its effects.
  • Elsewhere, look for your national health service or public health body.

Those sources are written by people whose job it is to get this right, and they're far better than a game's blog for anything medical.

Legal drinking age

Split the G with alcohol is for people of legal drinking age where they live. That age varies between countries, and pubs are legally required to enforce it. If you're under age, or just not drinking, there's a sparkling edition of the game that plays exactly the same with sparkling water. The app asks your age on first use, and if you say you're under age it offers the sparkling edition rather than shutting you out.

Getting home

Plan this before you go out, not at closing time when everyone's tired and the phone is on four percent. Know which bus or train you're catching, book a taxi in advance if it'll be busy, or agree who's walking with whom.

And never drive after drinking. Not after a couple, not after "just the one", not because it's only round the corner. Drink-driving limits vary from country to country, some are very low, and the only reliable way to stay safe and legal is not to drink at all if you're driving. The designated driver can still play, with water or an alcohol-free drink, and frankly deserves the first round of crisps.

Look after each other

The best pub groups keep half an eye on each other without making a thing of it. Some small habits:

  • Arrive together, leave together, or at least know how everyone's getting home.
  • Keep an eye on drinks. Don't leave a glass unattended with strangers around.
  • If someone's had enough, help them stop gracefully. A glass of water and a sit-down beats a public telling-off.
  • Make sure nobody heads off alone in a state where they shouldn't.

Respect people who aren't drinking

There are a hundred reasons someone might not drink tonight, and they don't owe the table any of them. "Go on, have one" is a phrase worth retiring. Pass them the phone, let them play with whatever's in their glass, and put their score on the same leaderboard as everyone else's.

Signs it's time to call it a night

You know these, but they're easy to ignore from the inside:

  • Your scores are getting worse and you've started blaming the camera.
  • You're struggling to hold the phone level or keep the glass steady.
  • Someone in the group is quiet in a way that isn't like them, or loud in a way that is.
  • You're thinking of ordering "one more for the game".

Any of those is a good moment to switch to water, get some food, or head home while the night is still a good memory.

The pub's side of things

Bar staff have a legal responsibility not to serve people who are drunk, and they can refuse service. That isn't them being difficult; it's their job, and in many places they can face real consequences for getting it wrong. If they say no, accept it with good grace. A bit of courtesy goes a long way with people who are working while you're relaxing. Some of that thinking is in Split the G etiquette.

Play it the way it's meant to be played

One fresh glass, one careful sip, one honest score. That's the whole game, and it's at its best when everyone at the table is still sharp enough to argue about the result. If you're of legal drinking age and fancy a go, open Split The G next time a pint lands in front of you. If not, the sparkling edition is waiting.
