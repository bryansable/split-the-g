Order a stout in a decent pub and there is a moment where the bartender pours most of it, puts it down on the drip tray and walks off to do something else. If you have never seen it before, it can look like they have forgotten about you. They have not. That pause is the whole point, and it is the difference between a pint that looks right and one that just looks wet.

What actually happens

The two-part pour goes roughly like this:

  1. The glass goes in at an angle. A clean, dry glass is held under the tap at about 45 degrees, with the beer aimed at the side of the glass rather than straight down into the bottom.
  2. The tap opens fully. Stout taps are designed to be opened all the way. Half-opening them gives a spluttery, uneven pour.
  3. The glass straightens as it fills. As the beer climbs, the glass is gradually brought upright, and the pour stops at around three quarters full. On a branded glass, that is usually somewhere around the bottom of the logo.
  4. It settles. The glass goes down and the beer does its thing: a cloudy, tan swirl of tiny bubbles drifting downwards while the dark beer clears from the bottom up and a head forms on top.
  5. The top-up. Once the beer below the head has gone fully dark and still, the glass goes back under the tap, upright this time, and is topped up so the head sits just proud of the rim, usually with the tap pushed the other way for a gentler flow.

Then it is handed over, ideally with a short pause so it can finish settling in front of you. If it is still swirling when it arrives, there is no shame in leaving it a moment.

Why bother splitting it in two?

Most draught stout in pubs is served with a gas mix that is mostly nitrogen, rather than the carbon dioxide used for lagers. Nitrogen does not dissolve into beer nearly as readily as carbon dioxide, so it forms tiny bubbles that come out of the beer quickly and dramatically when it is forced through the tap. That is what gives you the famous cascade and the thick, creamy head. There is a fuller explanation in why stout is so creamy.

If you pour all of it in one go, those bubbles have nowhere sensible to go. You end up with a glass that is mostly foam at first, which then sinks, leaving an underfilled pint with a slumped, uneven head. The first pour lets the gas come out of the beer and build a firm head. The second fills the glass back up and tightens that head into a neat, dense cap. It is less a ritual and more a practical way of dealing with how the gas behaves.

About that famous number

You may have heard that the perfect pint takes 119.5 seconds. That figure comes from the brewer's own marketing, and it is a good bit of marketing, but it is best treated as a rough guide rather than a law of physics. How long the settle takes depends on the temperature of the beer, the gas, the tap and the glass. A good bartender watches the beer, not the clock. If it takes a bit longer in a cold cellar, it takes a bit longer.

What a bad pour looks like

You do not need to be an expert to spot a pint that has been rushed. A few giveaways:

  • A huge, bubbly head. Big, visible bubbles on top, rather than a tight, smooth cream, usually mean the beer was poured in one go or the glass was not clean.
  • A head that is too thin. A barely-there layer suggests the glass was topped up before it settled, or that something is off with the gas.
  • Still cloudy when it arrives. The beer should be dark and clear below the head. If it is still tan and swirling, it has been served before it finished.
  • Bubbles clinging to the inside of the glass. Lots of large bubbles stuck to the walls are often a sign the glass was not properly clean.
  • Spilt down the side. A bit of overflow happens. A sticky, dripping glass suggests nobody cared much.

Why it matters if you want to split the G

This is where pouring and playing meet. When you try to split the G, you are judging where the boundary between the beer and the head ends up after one sip. If the pint has not finished settling, that boundary is still moving. You might leave the line bang on the bar of the G, set the glass down, and watch it creep downwards as the last of the bubbles rise and the head compacts.

A well-poured, fully settled pint gives you a sharp, stable line between dark beer and pale cream. That is easier for you to read, easier for your friends to argue about, and much easier for a camera. When you score a sip with Split The G, the app reads the liquid line at the front of the glass, measured as a fraction of the glass height between rim and base. A crisp boundary gives it a clean reading. A murky, half-settled one gives it something less certain, the same as it does for a human judge.

So if you are playing, give the pint the time it needs. You were going to wait for it anyway.

How to judge a pint as a customer, politely

Being able to tell a good pint from a rushed one is useful. Being the person who lectures the bar staff about it is not. A few thoughts on getting it right:

  • Be patient at the bar. If the bartender leaves your pint to settle, let them. Hovering or asking if it is ready does not speed up the gas.
  • Do not ask them to skip the settle. If you are in a hurry, order something that pours in one go.
  • Raise a genuinely bad pint quietly. If something is obviously wrong, like a flat, sour or very cloudy beer, a polite word is fair. Most bars would much rather swap it than have you sit there unhappy.
  • Accept that busy bars are busy. On a packed Saturday, a pint that is very slightly short of perfect is still a pint. Save your standards for the slow afternoons.
