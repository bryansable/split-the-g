Every group has one. The friend who claims they split the G every time, always when nobody was looking. A G-off is how you settle it: a proper, refereed evening where everybody gets the same shot, the same judge and the same rules. Done well, it is one of the best low-effort nights out going. Done badly, it is six people arguing over a blurry photo while the bar staff quietly despair. Here is how to do it well.

A quick word before the fun stuff. This is a game for adults of legal drinking age, and it is a game about one sip, not about how much anyone drinks. If any of your group are driving, not drinking, or just fancy a lighter night, the sparkling edition (same game, sparkling water, no alcohol) means they can play on exactly equal terms. More on that below.

The kit

You need less than you think.

  • One phone as the official judge. Open Split The G in the browser on whichever phone has the best camera and the most battery. You can add it to the home screen as a web app if you like; there is no app store download.
  • Glasses that match. Ideally everyone uses the same shape. The app measures everything as a fraction of the glass height, so mixed glasses still get a fair reading, but a tulip and a straight glass need different sized sips to reach the same mark. If you want a level field, standardise the glass.
  • A plain backdrop. A bare wall, a menu board turned over, a sheet of paper propped against a napkin holder. Busy backgrounds make the camera's job harder.
  • Water on the table. Non-negotiable. A jug and some glasses.
  • Something to eat. Crisps at the very least. Proper food is better.

Set up the judging spot

Pick one spot on the table and make it the stage. Put the glass there, phone at roughly the same height as the glass, a little way back so the whole glass is in frame from rim to base. Shooting from slightly above looks natural but it hides the near edge of the liquid; level is better. The app finds the rim, base and walls on its own, and if it guesses wrong you can drag the RIM and BASE guides into place. Do a test run with an untouched glass before anyone plays, so the judge knows the setup works.

Appoint a human judge as well, someone who holds the phone, keeps the order of play, and has the final word on procedure. Rotate the role if you like, so nobody misses out.

The rules

Keep them short enough to say out loud in thirty seconds.

  1. One sip. Lift, drink once, put the glass down. No second go, no topping up the sip.
  2. Hands off. Once the glass is down on the judging spot, nobody touches it. No tilting, no nudging, no "just straightening it".
  3. The judge reads it. The judge points the phone, the app reads the liquid line at the front of the glass, waits for a steady reading, and the judge taps capture. Whatever the screen says, stands.
  4. Let it settle. With stout, wait until the pour has fully settled before anyone sips. Everyone should start from the same kind of glass.

If anyone in your group has not played before, send them the guide to the perfect first sip on the way to the pub. It saves a lot of explaining.

Scoring

The app does the arithmetic. Land the line inside the bar of the G and you score 100. Miss it and you lose 6 points for every percent of glass height you were off. Scores of 85 and above are "So close", 60 and up a "Decent sip", and anything under 60 is a "Rookie pour". If you want the details, here is how the scoring works in plain language.

Write scores down, or let the app's history keep track if everyone is playing on their own phone in between official rounds.

Formats that work

Round robin

Everyone plays once per round, best total across the night wins. Simple, inclusive, nobody knocked out early to sulk by the fruit machine.

Knockout

Pair people up, higher score goes through. Dramatic, but with small groups it ends fast, and those knocked out have nothing to do. Better for bigger crowds.

Best of three across the evening

This is the one we would recommend. Three rounds spread across a few hours, with proper breaks in between for food, chat and water. Each player's best single score counts. It rewards consistency without rushing anyone, and it naturally paces the night: one drink per round, at the speed normal people drink.

Teams

Split into pairs or small teams and add up, or average, scores. Teams take the edge off for nervous players and let a drinker and a sparkling-water player share a side without anyone feeling left out.

Handicaps

If one of your group is clearly a ringer, level it up. A few ideas:

  • Give newcomers a flat bonus of five or ten points per round.
  • Make the champion use a different glass shape from everyone else. A wide-topped glass punishes overconfidence.
  • Let the previous round's winner go first, with nobody's attempt to learn from.

Tie-breaks

Ties happen, especially at 100. Every result also tells you how many millimetres the line landed too high or too low on a standard pint, so use that as the tie-break: closest to the middle wins. If two players both split it cleanly, call it a shared win rather than forcing another drink. Nobody needs a sudden-death round.

Use the daily G-off

If your friends are not all in one room, the app's friends feature fills the gap. Send an invite link, people sign in with an email magic link, and everyone's best score of the day lands on the daily G-off leaderboard, which resets at midnight. You can also text a challenge to someone straight from a result, or share the result card image to the group chat. It is a nice way to include the friend who moved away, or to settle things with the pub across town.

Keep it sensible

A G-off works because it is slow. One pint per round. Rounds spaced well apart. Water between drinks. Food early, not as an afterthought. Anyone who wants to switch to sparkling water mid-evening can, and their scores count exactly the same. Nobody gets pressured to drink, and nobody is made to finish a pint to "reset" the glass. Our guide to pub games and drinking responsibly has more on keeping game nights in proportion.

Make sure everybody has a safe way home before the first round, not after the last.

Be good to the venue

If you are hosting in a pub, have a word with the staff first, at a quiet moment. Most will be happy, a few will want you away from the bar. Take a table, not the counter. Do not ask for re-pours because someone missed. Tidy up your empties, thank the people serving you, and leave it in a state that means they will welcome you back.
