There is a particular look a bartender gives when a group of six has been standing at the taps for ten minutes, phones raised, discussing camera angles. You do not want to be on the receiving end of it. Split the G is a lovely little ritual, and most pubs are perfectly happy to see people enjoying a pint properly. The trouble starts when the game takes over the room. A few simple habits keep you on the right side of the people pouring your drinks, and the people drinking next to you.

If you are new to the whole thing, here is what Split the G actually is. Otherwise, read on.

The pour is not part of your game

The bartender's job is to pour you a good drink. It is not their job to give you a favourable starting position. So:

  • Do not ask for a re-pour because the head looks too thick, too thin, or "not right for splitting". A properly poured stout takes time already, and staff have other customers. If you want to understand why it takes a while, the two-part pour explains it.
  • Do not ask them to pour to a particular level. The pint is the pint.
  • Do not hover. Order, step back, and let them finish the settle and top-up without an audience breathing over the drip tray.

And when they hand it over, say thank you. Where tipping is customary, tip. A pint that took a careful two stages to pour deserves a bit of acknowledgement.

Keep the bar clear

The bar is where people order. It is not a photo studio. Collect your drink, take it to a table or a shelf, and do the game there. If there is nowhere to sit, find a corner away from the service point. Six people angling phones at the counter on a Friday night is a genuine obstruction, and you are holding up everyone queuing behind you.

The same goes for your own setup. If you need a plain background for the camera, a folded beer mat or a menu propped up on your own table does the job. Do not rearrange the pub's furniture or borrow the specials board.

Do not waste the drink

A sip that misses is still a sip of something you paid for and somebody brewed. The game is one mouthful, then you carry on enjoying the rest at your own pace. Nobody should be tipping a pint away to "reset", ordering an extra glass purely to have another go, or knocking back the remainder so they can play again sooner. That is not the spirit of the thing, and it is not a sensible way to drink either.

No pressure, ever

Not everyone at the table will be drinking, and that should make no difference to whether they can join in. Some will be driving, some will not drink at all, some will simply have had enough for the night. Never push anyone to have "just one for the game".

The sparkling edition exists for exactly this. Same game, same scoring, a glass of sparkling water instead. It counts the same on the leaderboard and nobody needs to explain themselves. Our guide to playing without alcohol has a few tips on getting a readable line from clear drinks. And it goes without saying: the stout version is for people of legal drinking age only.

Leave strangers alone

You will occasionally see someone at the next table take the first sip of a stout and land it well wide of the G. Let it go. They may not be playing. They may never have heard of the game. They are almost certainly not interested in your analysis. Heckling a stranger's sip is the fastest way to turn a friendly bit of fun into an awkward evening.

If somebody asks what you are up to, by all means explain and invite them in. Plenty of good pub conversations start that way. But wait to be asked.

Ask before you photograph people

When you point a camera at a glass in a crowded room, there are usually faces in the background. Most people do not want to appear in a stranger's share card. Frame the shot tight on the glass, use a plain backdrop, and if someone is clearly in view, either move or ask. The same applies to friends: check before you post a picture of someone mid-sip, especially in a group chat they are not part of.

For what it is worth, Split The G analyses camera frames on the phone itself and does not upload them. But anything you choose to share afterwards is your responsibility, so share thoughtfully.

Win quietly, lose gracefully

Splitting the G is a nice moment. Enjoy it. A quiet nod, a raised glass, perhaps a screenshot. What is less welcome is a lap of honour around the pub. Equally, if you miss, take it with a smile. The glass was not rigged, the camera was not lying, and the bartender did not sabotage you. You took a slightly small sip. It happens to everyone.

A good loser is welcome at any table. A bad loser, demanding replays and blaming the light, gets quietly left off the next invite.

Settling disputes

Eyeballing a pint is a recipe for arguments. Was that line on the bar of the G or just under it? From where you are sitting it looks one way, from across the table another. Perspective genuinely plays tricks here, because the back edge of the liquid looks higher or lower than the front depending on where your eyes are.

This is where an impartial judge helps. Agree before you start that whatever the app says, stands. Put the glass down, nobody touches it, one person points the phone level with the glass and lets it read the line at the front. It waits for a steady reading, you capture it, and the score is the score. If two people tie, the millimetre feedback tells you who was closer. No appeals, no recounts.

A short checklist

  • Order, thank, move away from the bar.
  • Never ask for a re-pour.
  • One sip, then enjoy the rest at a normal pace.
  • Offer the sparkling edition to anyone not drinking, and do not make a thing of it.
  • Keep strangers out of shot and out of the commentary.
  • Let the app settle arguments.
  • Leave your table tidy and the staff glad you came.

Give it a go

Next time you have a pint in front of you, find a quiet corner, open Split The G on your phone, and score your sip. Then put the phone away and enjoy the drink. That is the whole game, and it is better for being small.
