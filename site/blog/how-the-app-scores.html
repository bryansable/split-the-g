When a phone tells you that you scored 76, it is fair to ask where that number came from. Nobody likes being marked by a black box, least of all after a sip they were quite pleased with. So here is what Split The G is actually doing when you point it at a glass, in plain language, along with what it cannot do and how to get the most honest reading out of it.

The big idea: everything is a fraction

A camera does not know how big your glass is. A pint held close fills the screen; the same pint across the table is a small shape in the middle. If the app measured in pixels, the score would depend on where you stood.

So it does not. It finds two reference points, the rim of the glass and the base, and treats the distance between them as the whole height, top to bottom. Every other measurement is expressed as a fraction of that height. The liquid line might be, say, 31% of the way down from the rim. That number is the same whether the phone is twenty centimetres away or a metre, and whether the glass is a full pint or a half. Distance and size simply drop out of the sum.

Finding the glass

When you point the camera at a glass with the whole thing in view, the app looks for the rim, the base and the two side walls. These give it the frame it needs: where the glass starts and ends, and where its front face is.

It usually gets this right on its own, but not always. A busy background, a shadow across the base, or a glass cut off at the edge of the frame can throw it. If the guides land in the wrong place, you can drag the RIM or BASE guide to where they should be. Since everything is measured relative to those two lines, getting them right matters more than anything else.

Where the G goes

On the classic tulip pint, the G sits about 30% of the way down from the rim. The app uses that to draw a "ghost G" on screen at the matching height on whatever glass you are holding. That is what lets you play with a nonic, a shaker or a jam jar if you are feeling adventurous. If you have the real branded glass, switch on the "Official glass" toggle and the ghost goes away so it does not sit on top of the printed letter. Our piece on the tulip pint glass explains why the letter sits where it does, and why the curve of the glass makes the sip larger than it looks.

Reading the front of the glass

After your sip, put the glass down. The app then reads the liquid line live, and it reads it at the front of the glass, the side facing the camera, which is where the G is printed.

That detail matters because of perspective. The surface of a drink is a flat circle. Look at it from exactly level and it collapses into a thin line. Look from even slightly above and the circle opens into an ellipse: the back edge appears higher up the glass than the front edge. The higher your camera, the bigger that gap. If the app read the back edge, or the middle, it would make your sip look smaller than it was.

The G is on the front, so the front is what counts. It is also why the best advice is to keep the phone level with the glass rather than looking down on it. A level camera keeps the near edge of the liquid crisp and stops the far edge muddling things.

Waiting for a steady reading

Liquids move. A glass that has just been put down sloshes a little, the head shifts, and your hand holding the phone is never perfectly still. So the app does not grab the first number it sees. It watches the reading over a moment and waits for it to settle before you capture. If you tap too eagerly while things are still moving, you are more likely to get a reading you will argue with. Give it a second.

The score

The bar in the middle of the G has some height of its own, and that band is the target. If the liquid line lands anywhere inside it, you score 100 and the app says "Split the G!".

Miss the band and you lose 6 points for every percent of glass height you are outside it. So being 2% of the glass height away costs you 12 points and leaves you on 88. Being 5% away costs 30, leaving 70. The penalty is the same whether you went too high or too low.

The results fall into tiers:

  • 100: Split the G!
  • 85 and above: So close
  • 60 and above: Decent sip
  • Below 60: Rookie pour

Six points per percent is deliberately firm. A couple of percent is a small distance on a glass, and the game is supposed to reward precision. But it is not so harsh that a near miss scores nothing.

The millimetre feedback

A percentage is a slightly abstract thing to learn from. So each result also converts your miss into millimetres on a standard pint and tells you which way you went. Something like "4 mm too low. Smaller sip next time." or the opposite if you were too timid.

This is the bit worth paying attention to. Most people miss in the same direction most of the time. If your history is full of "too high", you are being polite with your sips and need to commit. If it says "too low", ease off. The app keeps a history of your scores with a chart, your best, your average and your streak, which makes these patterns easy to spot. Privacy: the camera stays on your phone

The camera frames are analysed on the phone itself. They are not uploaded anywhere. What you get out is a score, and if you choose to, a result card image you can share or a challenge you can text to a friend. If you sign in to play with friends on the daily leaderboard, it is your scores that are shared, not footage of your kitchen.

What it cannot do

No camera reading is perfect, and it is better to be honest about the limits.

  • It needs the whole glass. If the rim or the base is out of shot, the fraction has nothing to be a fraction of.
  • Busy backgrounds confuse it. Patterned wallpaper, a crowded bar and bright lights behind the glass all make edges harder to find.
  • Steep angles distort the line. Shooting from above is the most common cause of a surprising score.
  • Some drinks are harder than others. A stout with a thick, dense head gives a sharp, high-contrast line and is the easiest to read. Clear drinks work too, since the app looks for the meniscus, the slight curve where the liquid meets the glass, but they need decent light.

Getting a good reading

  1. Set the glass against a plain background: a wall, a menu, a sheet of paper.
  2. Hold the phone level with the glass, a little way back, with the entire glass in view.
  3. Check the rim and base guides and drag them if they are off.
  4. Take your one sip, put the glass down and take your hands away.
  5. Wait for the reading to steady, then tap capture.

On a desktop, you can use "Pick photo" to score a picture of a glass instead of using the live camera. The same rules apply: whole glass in frame, level angle, plain background. Our guide to photographing a pint covers lighting and angles in more depth.

See it for yourself

The best way to understand the scoring is to watch it work. Open Split The G in your phone's browser, line up a glass and see where the ghost G lands. If you are of legal drinking age, try it with a stout; if not, or if you would rather not, the sparkling edition works just the same with sparkling water. One sip, one honest number.
