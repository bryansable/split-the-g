Most pint photos are bad. Not because the pint was bad, but because they were taken in a hurry, from above, under a yellow bulb, with a thumbprint on the lens and somebody's coat in the background. That is fine if all you want is a record that you were in a pub. It is less fine if you want a camera to read where the liquid line sits to the nearest millimetre, or if you want a picture that people actually stop scrolling for.

The good news is that the two goals overlap almost completely. Whatever helps a scoring app see the glass clearly also helps a human eye. So here is a practical guide to photographing a pint, written by someone who has spent more time than is strictly dignified crouched at table height in dim bars.

Start with the light, because the pub won't help

Pub lighting is designed for atmosphere, not photography. It tends to be low, warm and coming from above, often from a row of small bulbs or a lamp that throws a hot spot right onto the rim of your glass. Your phone will cope, but it will cope by raising the sensitivity, which adds grain and softens edges. Soft edges are exactly what you don't want when the thing you care about is a thin line between dark stout and pale head.

If you have any choice about where you sit, pick a spot near a window during the day. Light coming from the side is the most flattering light a pint can get. It rakes across the glass, shows the texture of the head, and makes the boundary between beer and foam crisp. Put the window to your left or right, not behind the glass.

That last point matters. Backlight, meaning a bright window or lamp directly behind the pint, is the classic mistake. The camera exposes for the bright background, the stout turns into a black silhouette, and the glass picks up a glare that can swallow the line completely. Shuffle round so the light is beside you. If it has to be behind you, fine, but watch for your own shadow falling across the glass.

At night, use what the bar gives you. Move the glass away from the harshest bulb, and if there is a candle or a table lamp, keep it to one side. Avoid the phone's flash. It flattens everything, bounces straight back off the glass and tends to make a stout look like a bottle of ink with a hat on.

A word on white balance

Warm pub light turns a creamy head orange. For scoring this hardly matters, because the contrast between dark and light is what counts. For a photo you want to share, it can look a bit nicotine-stained. Most phone cameras let you tap to focus and then adjust exposure; some let you nudge the colour temperature too. If yours doesn't, a gentle cool-down in your editor afterwards usually brings the head back to something nearer white. Don't overdo it. A pint should look like it lives in a pub, not a laboratory.

Camera height: level with the line

This is the single most useful habit to build. Hold the phone level with the part of the glass you care about, not above it looking down.

When you shoot from above, the rim of the glass becomes an ellipse and the liquid surface becomes another ellipse, and the line you want to measure smears across a band rather than sitting neatly as a horizontal stroke. You also see the top of the head rather than the side of the glass, which hides the very thing you are trying to judge. Bring the phone down so the lens sits roughly at the height of the liquid. The front edge of the line then reads as a clean, flat stripe.

For Split The G this is the difference between a confident reading and a wobbly one. The app reads the liquid line at the front of the glass, and that front edge is clearest when you're looking at it straight on. Rest the phone on the table edge or a beer mat stack if it helps.

Distance and framing

Get the whole glass in frame, rim to base, with a little breathing room above and below. It is tempting to fill the screen with the G and the head, but the app needs to see both ends of the glass. It finds the rim, the base and the walls, then measures everything as a fraction of the height between rim and base. That is why the distance from the glass and the size of the glass don't matter, and also why chopping off the base does.

A good rule is to stand back far enough that the glass takes up somewhere around half to two-thirds of the screen height. Close enough for detail, far enough that nothing gets cropped. If the app's rim or base guide lands in the wrong place, perhaps because a coaster edge or a reflection confused it, you can drag the RIM or BASE guide to where it should be. That fixes the maths without you having to reshoot.

Background: plain beats pretty

The back bar of a good pub is a lovely thing. Rows of bottles, brass taps, mirrors, fairy lights. It is also a nightmare behind a glass, because every one of those shapes competes with the edges of your pint. Mirrors are the worst: they reflect the room and sometimes the glass itself.

Turn your pint so it sits against something plain. A painted wall, a wooden panel, the back of a booth, even a menu held up by a patient friend. Darker plain backgrounds make a pale head pop; lighter ones outline the dark body of a stout. Either is better than busy. If you are playing with something clear, a plain background matters even more, which we cover in playing without alcohol.

Steady hands

Low light means slower shutter speeds, and slower shutter speeds punish shaky hands. Rest your elbows on the table. Hold the phone in both hands. Breathe out before you tap. If you are scoring, the app reads live, so give it a moment to settle on the line rather than stabbing the capture button the instant the glass touches down. Let the liquid stop moving too; a sloshing surface is not a fair reading.

The boring bits that ruin photos

Clean your lens

Phones live in pockets and pockets are full of fluff. A smeared lens gives everything a dreamy glow that looks like haze around every highlight. Give it a wipe on a clean bit of shirt before you start. It takes two seconds and fixes more bad pub photos than any editing app.

Condensation

A cold glass in a warm room fogs up. That fog sits right where the line is and blurs it. A quick wipe of the outside of the glass with a napkin, just around the front, clears it. Do it before the sip, so you aren't fiddling with the glass afterwards.

Reflections

Glass reflects. Bright windows, ceiling lights and your own phone screen all show up as streaks. Tilt the phone a few degrees or move a step to one side and watch the streak slide off the part you care about. You rarely need to eliminate every reflection, just the one sitting across the line.

Making a photo worth sharing

Once the score is in, the photo becomes a bragging rights document. A few touches help. Keep the glass slightly off-centre, with a bit of the table in front for context. Let the background fall a little out of focus if your phone does that naturally. Make sure the G and the line are sharp, because that is the whole story.

When you share a result card from the app, there is a crop toggle. Turned on, it trims the photo tight to the glass so the line and the score are the focus; turned off, you keep the surroundings, which is nice if the pub itself is part of the memory. Try both and see which tells the better story. And if you are curious why the reading came out as it did, how the app scores your sip explains the numbers.

One last thing worth knowing: the camera frames are analysed on your phone and never uploaded. The photo only leaves your hands if you choose to share it.

Give it a go

Next time you have a pint in front of you, try it properly: side light, phone level, plain wall, clean lens, whole glass in view. Then score your sip with Split The G and see how much sharper the reading feels. If you're of legal drinking age, enjoy the pint at your own pace; if you're not drinking, the sparkling edition plays the same game with water.
